# B — Product discovery: your friend has a great idea

**Candidate brief.** A friend has an idea and wants you to help turn it into a product. Before committing to building it, work out whose problem it might solve, what could complicate it, and what you would need to learn.

Choose **one** idea below. You may recommend proceeding, narrowing the idea, changing direction, or not pursuing it. There is no preferred verdict.

Timebox the assignment to **3–5 hours**, including research, the artifact, and notes; stop by five hours. Show how you would move through discovery, but **full completion is not expected**. An honest thinking log is more valuable than covering every step without transparency. Record attempts, uncertainties, changes of mind, and unfinished work. We do not expect weeks of research or contact with real participants during the assignment.

## Idea 1 — memories in a familiar voice or image

Your friend proposes a service that creates new materials from a voice sample or photo of someone who has died:

- Audiobooks in a deceased parent's voice for children who lost that parent.
- Animated “live photos” or short videos made from existing photos.
- Memorial materials for soldiers who have died.

These are proposed use cases, not validated demand or evidence of benefit. Choose a specific audience and use case to investigate; you do not need to pursue all three.

## Idea 2 — a platform for dog owners

Your friend proposes a platform for dog owners interested in gluten-free, lactose-free, or “healthy” food for their dogs. It could become a marketplace.

The audience labels, meaning of “healthy,” need for these diets, and marketplace format are assumptions to examine. Decide what problem is worth investigating before selecting a business model.

## Constraint: imagine you have €200 in cash

You have **€200 total** for discovery and the first validation experiments over four weeks, with no promised additional funding. Assume you can contribute up to ten hours per week without drawing a salary; make any other staffing assumptions explicit. This is a hypothetical budget: do not spend your own money.

Account for recruitment, participant compensation, specialist advice, tools, prototype costs, acquisition tests, and contingencies where relevant. Not every category needs spending. Free resources are acceptable if access and effort are credible.

Give estimated costs and assumptions; do not invent supplier quotes. Show the total, reserve or unspent balance, sequence of spending, and evidence required before releasing the next portion. If meaningful validation cannot fit the budget, explain why and propose a narrower question or a stop decision.

## What to produce

Show the discovery loop in concise notes plus a rough artifact:

1. **Frame the opportunity.** Separate the friend's proposed solution from a possible problem. Identify the user, payer, other affected people, existing alternatives, and assumptions.
2. **Find initial evidence.** Do a small amount of desk research if useful. Cite sources and distinguish findings from hypotheses. Existing products do not by themselves prove demand. If you cannot verify something, say so.
3. **Plan research.** Explain whom you would recruit, how, and why; include several non-leading questions about past behavior. Describe how you would synthesize findings and handle conflicting evidence. Real interviews are not required; hypothetical quotes must not be presented as research.
4. **Investigate complications.** Prioritize the uncertainties that could invalidate the idea or change the research method. For each major risk, state what you need to learn, from whom or what source, and how the answer would change your plan.
5. **Choose a focus.** Select a narrow opportunity, justify it, and explain what you are deferring. “Do not build yet” is a valid choice.
6. **Explore alternatives.** Compare at least three distinct responses to the problem, including a simpler or non-product approach. Do not assume a marketplace or generated media is necessary.
7. **Design the first experiment.** Identify the riskiest assumption, participant group, method, observation, cost, and limitations. Explain what the test cannot establish.
8. **Make an artifact.** Produce a storyboard, interview guide with a synthesis example, landing-page sketch, service workflow, or low-fidelity prototype that demonstrates the test. Label simulated content; a production implementation is unnecessary.
9. **Set decision criteria and next steps.** Define proposed proceed/change/stop thresholds and justify them. Allocate the €200 across a staged plan, explain what happens after each result, and state what remains unknown.

Include the shared decision and AI/tool log. We value your interpretation, choices, and checks more than a polished deck.

## Complications to consider

These prompts are starting points for investigation, not conclusions or a requirement to write an exhaustive compliance checklist.

### For the memorial idea

Who should be consulted about using a person's likeness or voice? What happens if family members disagree or the deceased person's wishes are unknown? What rights, permissions, and provider terms would need specialist verification?

How might reactions differ across adults, children, and grieving families? Who could help design appropriate research, and what would make you pause it? How would you avoid treating enthusiasm from one relative as evidence of benefit to a child?

What source recordings are available and usable? How would you investigate quality, privacy, retention, misuse, authenticity, generation costs, and trust? How would you distinguish reconstructed material from an original memory? Do the audiobook, photo animation, and soldier-memorial use cases require different approaches?

For this assignment, use fictional or clearly labelled placeholder media. Do not clone a real deceased person's voice, use private family media, or recruit bereaved children. Explain how appropriate involvement might be planned with qualified support; that work is outside the take-home.

### For the dog-food idea

Do owners' preferences correspond to a demonstrated problem for their dogs? How would you distinguish taste, branding, diagnosed needs, and a vague desire for healthier food? What expertise and evidence would you seek before making nutritional claims?

Who chooses, pays, and advises? What do owners currently buy, why, and where? What would they switch from, and what would make them trust your offering?

If you propose a marketplace, how would you investigate both buyer and seller demand, supply quality, fulfillment, repeat purchase, acquisition costs, margins, and responsibility for claims? What cheaper test could precede building the marketplace?

The assignment does not require dietary advice, feeding trials, or unsupported health claims. Identify questions that need qualified input and show how to investigate them.

## Interview follow-up — organizers only

Use one **hypothetical new fact**, not an additional real research finding:

- Memorial: “One relative wants to proceed, another objects, and nobody knows the deceased person's wishes. How does this affect your next experiment?”
- Dog food: “Interviewed owners like the branding, but their current purchases do not match the stated preferences. What would you test next?”
- Either idea: “Your available cash is now €50. What do you preserve, cut, or stop, and why?”

Ask the candidate to revise a concrete part of the artifact or budget. Look for evidence-based judgment, research design, recognition of complications, realistic spending, and willingness to reconsider.
